# Development challenges, corrective actions and lessons learned

*Maintained development record through the final paper assembly. All ten analytical notebooks and the assembled paper are complete within the documented scope; the unresolved empirical validation fold and other limitations remain explicit.*

Recovering a density from option prices required more than implementing the valuation relationship. The development work exposed difficulties in numerical differentiation, data interpretation, consistency across maturities and the estimation of unobserved tails. This section records the observed problems, the responses tested, the evidence used to assess those responses and the limitations that remain. It distinguishes unexpected numerical problems from deliberately constructed stress tests. An unsuccessful experiment is retained where it explains why the method changed.

The account follows the ten notebooks so that each decision can be traced to its calculations. Equation, figure, table and reference numbers refer to the shared project registers. Numerical outcomes below are results of this project; the cited literature supports the underlying relationships and methods, rather than independently validating these particular results.

## 1. Notebook 1: differentiating clean prices and prices with errors

As Bahra explains in his Bank of England working paper, the curvature of European call prices with respect to strike reveals the risk-neutral terminal density under the stated assumptions [1, §§2.1–2.2]. This relationship, attributed originally to Breeden and Litzenberger, supplies Equation (3). Its analytical validity did not ensure that a finite-difference implementation would behave well at every strike.

An initial grid beginning at 1,000 index points produced tiny negative density estimates in the far-left tail of the clean lognormal example. In this region the call-price curve was nearly linear, so its second difference required subtracting large, almost cancelling quantities. As Driscoll and Braun discuss in *Fundamentals of Numerical Computation*, differentiation can expose the competition between approximation error and floating-point roundoff [7, §§5.4–5.5]. The observed negative values were therefore investigated as a numerical issue rather than interpreted as negative probabilities in the analytical model.

For the supplied parameters, the validated grid was moved to 2,500–14,000 with a spacing of five index points. The interior differentiated points then ran from 2,505 to 13,995. This change was assessed through retained probability mass, the mean–forward relationship, density error and option repricing. The recovered mass was approximately 0.9999999985 and the integrated absolute density error was approximately $2.91\times10^{-6}$. No clipping or renormalisation was used. Figure 3 also showed approximately second-order convergence over the tested spacings. The lesson was to validate a boundary decision against quantities with known answers: these particular boundaries are a result for this benchmark, not a universal rule for market data.

A separate, deliberate stress test added independent quote errors with a standard deviation of 0.05 index points. Approximately 34.67% of the recovered density values became negative. This was not a recurrence of the clean-data boundary problem. The perturbations were allowed to violate the price-shape restrictions, and Equation (4) amplified them through division by squared strike spacing. Driscoll and Braun's second-difference stencil explains that algebraic amplification [7]; it does not make this experiment a calibrated market-noise model. Figure 4 therefore motivated fitting a constrained price or probability representation before attempting density interpretation. It also established a practical lesson: reducing the grid spacing can improve a clean-data calculation while making a noisy-data calculation less useful.

Evidence: [Notebook 1 methodology](methodology.md), [clean-data validation](../results/validation.json) and [noise diagnostic](../results/noise_diagnostic.json), alongside Figures 3–4. The Figure 4 explanation remains beneath its figure and caption in the notebook.

## 2. Notebook 2: obtaining quotes did not establish their suitability

The first data-access setback was that the attempted Cboe DataShop sample download returned HTTP 403. The sample's contents were consequently not inspected. The available product documentation could describe a potential source but could not establish the properties of an unavailable file. A public Cboe quote page provided an alternative route to a candidate snapshot through its embedded data payload. The response was preserved with provenance and a checksum before analysis. This allowed the data audit to proceed without treating the unsuccessful sample download as completed access.

The alternative payload contained 27,782 records across 61 root-and-expiry groups, but availability introduced further questions. All bid and ask size fields were zero, and the available quote-time field did not establish a full observation date and timezone. The underlying index's last-trade timestamp was a different field and could not safely substitute for the option observation time. Likewise, a retrieval timestamp established when the file was collected, not when each quote was observed. An expiry date alone did not determine the contract's precise settlement horizon.

Cboe's contract and data specifications describe the fields and conventions that a suitable dataset should provide [2]–[5]. They do not supply missing information in a different payload. The audit therefore retained SPX and SPXW separately, preserved every row with its screening decisions and avoided interpreting zero size as confirmed market illiquidity. A total of 25,866 rows passed the chosen 25% relative-spread price screen, but this was a local arithmetic screen, not approval for calibration. The saved report explicitly retained `density_estimation_ready: false`.

The response was to continue the numerical research with controlled synthetic observations while keeping the empirical limitation visible. That decision preserved progress without introducing an assumed quote date, settlement convention or forward. The lesson was that a plausible-looking option chain is not yet an evidential basis for an empirical density. The unresolved metadata and quote-field questions remain requirements for the market-data stage; this notebook did not solve them by finding another download route.

## 3. Notebook 3: replacing unstable differentiation with a constrained fit

The quote-noise experiment created a methodological problem: a useful estimator needed to respect probability constraints while tolerating imperfect prices. As Aït-Sahalia and Duarte explain in “Nonparametric option pricing under shape restrictions,” restrictions on the option-price curve provide economically meaningful information for estimation [9, §2]. This motivated a constrained representation, although their particular estimation algorithm was not implemented here.

The project instead represented the density by nonnegative interval probabilities, with unit total mass and a mean equal to the supplied forward. Exact integration of the call payoff within each interval produced Equation (11), avoiding an additional numerical-differentiation step. As Boyd and Vandenberghe discuss in *Convex Optimization*, a regularised fitting objective can balance agreement with observations against a penalty on irregularity [11, §§4.4, 6.3]. Equation (12) applied that framework to the interval probabilities. The scientific change was in what was estimated and constrained; it was not a post-processing operation that removed negative density values.

With 81 synthetic prices and 120 intervals, the declared baseline gave a withheld clean-price root-mean-square error (RMSE) of approximately 0.246113 index points and a density error of approximately 0.030998 on the selected support. The fitted call curve passed the reported monotonicity and convexity checks. However, mass and forward consistency were imposed in the optimisation, so their satisfaction could not independently demonstrate accurate density recovery. The comparison with the known density and with withheld clean prices provided separate evidence.

The three smoothing choices in Figure 9 exposed an additional limitation: a feasible estimate still depended on regularisation. The middle setting was an illustrative declared baseline, not a parameter selected by minimising error against the known answer. No unresolved solver failure was reported for this notebook. Its contribution to the development record is the response to noisy differentiation and the recognition that feasibility, price accuracy and density accuracy must be evaluated separately. Support and smoothing choices remained research questions for subsequent notebooks.

Evidence: [constrained-fit methodology](constrained_methodology.md) and [validation for all three penalties](../results/constrained_validation.json), including Figures 7–9 and Tables 5–6.

## 4. Notebook 4: individually valid densities formed an inconsistent family

Extending the estimator to eight horizons exposed a restriction that the single-maturity experiment could not test. Each marginal fit satisfied its probability constraints, yet the collection failed the calendar-consistency diagnostic. As Gatheral and Jacquier explain in “Arbitrage-free SVI volatility surfaces,” calendar ordering is assessed in appropriate forward coordinates under the relevant carry assumptions [12, §2.1]. Equations (13)–(14) therefore compared forward-normalised call values at fixed forward moneyness, rather than comparing unadjusted call prices at the same numerical strike.

The independent fits produced 2,762 decreases exceeding the stated tolerance in 13,307 adjacent-maturity grid comparisons. All occurred outside the corresponding shared quote ranges; none occurred among the 4,267 comparisons inside those ranges. The known analytical benchmark passed the same diagnostic. This indicated an extrapolation and joint-consistency problem in the recovered collection, even though each individual density had passed its marginal checks. These are correlated numerical comparisons, not counts of independent market trading opportunities.

The surface was retained and labelled as inconsistent across maturities, allowing the next notebook to address the observed failure directly. The lesson was that valid marginals do not automatically constitute a valid family across time. In particular, the visual plausibility of a smooth-looking three-dimensional surface did not replace a diagnostic based on option values.

The interactive presentation also required care. A three-dimensional view can obscure local features, and connecting facets can suggest estimates at horizons that were never fitted. The explorer therefore paired rotation, zoom and maturity controls with an exact two-dimensional histogram slice, fixed scales and a known-versus-fitted comparison. The display window was cropped without renormalising the probabilities. Its plotting dependency was included locally, and the controls were checked with network access disabled. The maturity sweep was explicitly distinguished from a historical-date animation. These choices made the visual comparison inspectable while preserving the limits of what the calculations established.

Evidence: [surface methodology](surface_methodology.md), [marginal and calendar diagnostics](../results/surface_validation.json), [offline browser checks](../results/surface_browser_checks.json) and the [first interactive companion](../interactive/density_surface.html).

## 5. Notebook 5: calendar constraints at bin boundaries were insufficient

The next response was to fit the maturities jointly with calendar inequalities. A comparison issue arose because the joint implementation used a different solver from the earlier independent fits. To isolate the effect of the new constraints, an independent control was fitted with the same OSQP solver on the same observations. As Stellato and co-authors describe in their OSQP paper, the solver's numerical stopping criteria and residuals are part of assessing the returned solution [13, §3.4]. Keeping solver and inputs aligned made the change in constraints assessable without attributing all numerical differences to joint fitting.

The first joint solve imposed calendar inequalities at the common bin boundaries. A more detailed check nevertheless found a negative calendar gap inside a bin: approximately $-3.14\times10^{-7}$, exceeding the published acceptance tolerance of $10^{-8}$. The corrective step used the estimator's own structure. Under uniform-bin probabilities the within-bin gap is quadratic, so its endpoints and any eligible interior minimum can be evaluated directly. Equations (18)–(19) derive those candidate locations from the interval-payoff formula. Offending locations were then added as constraints before refitting.

Five solve-and-check rounds increased the calendar-constraint count from 833 to 858 and reduced the minimum gap to approximately $-8.56\times10^{-10}$. A separate constructed example demonstrated why this mattered: two non-lognormal distributions passed every boundary comparison while having an interior gap of $-0.009375$ at normalised strike 0.875. The checker detected that failure, and the constrained solver removed it to tolerance. This provided a targeted test of the interval logic beyond the main lognormal example. It also showed why adding more uniformly spaced checks would have been a weaker verification strategy than using the known piecewise structure.

The controlled comparison had 2,763 sampled violations before the calendar constraints and zero afterwards at the published tolerance. Pooled withheld-price RMSE changed only slightly, from 0.119513 to 0.119153 index points, while mean density error increased slightly from 0.035158 to 0.035162. Figure 13 still showed a discrepancy in the upper tail. The result therefore established a consistency improvement, not a general improvement in density accuracy. Small signed numerical residuals were retained and disclosed, and the checks applied to adjacent fitted horizons rather than to a continuous-time interpolation.

The main lessons were to inspect constraints between grid points, use a matched control when changing the estimator and distinguish satisfaction of restrictions from recovery of the underlying distribution. The remaining tail discrepancy supplied a concrete reason for the sensitivity study in Notebook 6.

## 6. Notebook 6: making sensitivity comparisons meaningful

### 6.1 Grid changes also changed the effective smoothing

A direct comparison of different bin counts initially risked changing two things at once: density resolution and the effective strength of smoothing. The raw coefficient in Equation (12) multiplies differences between density heights. It therefore has a different interpretation when the interval width changes. As Driscoll and Braun's second-derivative stencil makes explicit, the same discrete second difference corresponds to different curvature at different spacings [7, §5.4.2]. Equations (21)–(22) used that relationship to define a grid-scaled roughness penalty and convert its coefficient back into the implementation's original convention.

The resolution comparisons consequently held the scaled coefficient fixed. The support comparisons used nested boundaries with the original spacing, separating a change in endpoints from a simultaneous change in interval width. This controlled the specified discrete penalty; it did not prove that different grids would produce identical estimators or that the chosen penalty was statistically optimal. The lesson was that a sensitivity study must first establish what is genuinely being held constant.

Changing support also exposed a weakness in the earlier error measure. An integral restricted to each fitted support would omit different amounts of known tail probability and could reward an artificially short domain. Equation (23) therefore added the benchmark probability outside the support, where the fitted density is zero. The within-support integral was checked with increasing quadrature orders and an adaptive fallback. This change made the error definition appropriate to the comparison, while requiring the new full-domain values to be distinguished from the earlier support-only measure.

### 6.2 A broader benchmark exposed solver limitations

The original single-lognormal example was supplemented with a mixture having weights 0.75 and 0.25 and component volatilities of 14% and 40%. As Bahra discusses in his treatment of mixtures, component distributions and their option values can be combined using mixture weights [1, §§3.5–3.6 and mathematical appendix]. The project's common component forwards and fixed weights across horizons supplied a known, consistent benchmark with a broader tail. The histogram estimator was not given those component weights or the known density. Both benchmark families used the same strike grids and paired quote errors.

This more demanding case revealed a numerical setback: the original OSQP formulation did not meet the required stopping rule for the mixture baseline. Increasing the iteration allowance from 100,000 to 300,000, while retaining the tolerances, did not resolve the problem. A starting point based on independent fits and trials with alternative positive objective scales also did not produce an accepted solution. An equivalent formulation with explicit price-error and roughness variables was tested with OSQP, but that change alone still failed to meet the stopping requirement. These attempts were not counted as successful fits.

As Goulart and Chen explain in their Clarabel research paper, an interior-point solver can handle convex quadratic objectives with conic constraints and assess feasibility and optimality through its stopping conditions [15, §§1, 2.6]. The explicit-residual formulation was therefore implemented with Clarabel while preserving the same objective and linear constraints. A small, independently specified comparison checked agreement with the earlier expanded quadratic formulation. Separate checks then examined the final probability weights, the residual-variable equalities and the calendar gaps. Because both solver and formulation changed, the outcome does not establish that a particular matrix product was the sole cause of the earlier difficulty, or that explicit residuals alone solved it.

A separate runtime difficulty arose when the initial automatic linear-solver configuration ran slowly. That unfinished run was interrupted; it was not evidence of infeasibility. Pinning the QDLDL linear solver and one solver thread, with single-thread numerical-library settings during generation, made the study practical to execute in the working environment. This was a computational configuration change, not a relaxation of the financial constraints or an established performance result for all hardware.

All 14 final fits used the same accepted Clarabel configuration and passed the separate checks across 112 marginal densities. The worst calendar minimum was approximately $-9.80\times10^{-10}$, within the $10^{-8}$ acceptance tolerance. Raw bin probabilities remained positive, and no clipping or renormalisation was applied. The earlier OSQP defaults were retained so that Notebook 5 remained reproducible. The lesson was to reject an inadequate solver status, test alternative formulations without changing the scientific objective, and validate the returned quantities independently of the success message.

### 6.3 Passing the checks still did not establish accurate tails

The completed study showed why pricing and density diagnostics were both needed. For the single-lognormal benchmark, changing from 80 to 160 bins barely changed pooled withheld-price RMSE, from approximately 0.119451 to 0.119123 index points, while mean full-domain density error changed from approximately 0.048880 to 0.028548. Agreement with withheld option prices did not uniquely establish the recovered density shape.

As Malz discusses in his New York Fed research paper, extrapolation choices and the placement of a tail threshold relative to available strikes matter when extracting distributional summaries [14, §§2.3, 3.2]. At 365 days, the threshold $1.8F$ lay above the largest supplied strike. The known mixture probability above this threshold was approximately 1.1885%, while the seven fitted settings gave approximately 1.7455%–2.7208%. Thus every tested setting overestimated this particular tail probability even though every final fit passed the calendar checks. In contrast, every setting underestimated the mixture's normalised terminal-level variance. Those two observations are compatible because a tail-event probability and a second moment weight the distribution differently.

The narrow-support mixture fit also placed approximately 1.63% of its mass in the final bin near the imposed upper endpoint. This explained the large upper-tail envelope spike in Figure 15. It was evidence of sensitivity to the support boundary, not an additional mode discovered in the known benchmark. Wider support reduced omitted benchmark probability but did not, by itself, establish accurate estimation throughout the unobserved tail.

The resulting lesson was to report known values alongside sensitivity ranges and to inspect several summaries of the distribution. The shaded envelopes and minimum-to-maximum ranges describe seven modelling choices; they are not confidence intervals. The experiment uses one paired quote-noise realisation and two benchmark families, so repeated-noise studies, different strike coverage and a separately evaluated tuning rule remain necessary before wider claims. Numerical consistency was achieved to the stated tolerance; robust tail recovery remains an open problem.

Evidence: [robustness methodology](robustness_methodology.md), [final-study solver histories and diagnostics](../results/robustness_validation.json), [exported probabilities](../results/robustness_bin_masses.csv), [independent numerical checks](../tests/test_robustness.py), Figures 15–17 and Tables 11–13. The final accepted runs are archived in full; the unsuccessful development trials are summarised above and are not included as fitted-study observations.

## 7. Notebook 7: separating repeated-noise effects from missing strike information

### 7.1 A fair coverage comparison required control of quote count

The earlier sensitivity study used one quote-noise realisation and one strike pattern. Extending that work introduced a design difficulty: simply narrowing a grid would change both the number of observations and their range. The response was to define four nested patterns before the full experiment. Original 81 retained the previous grid, Sparse 41 kept every second original strike, Central 41 retained the central half of that grid, and Extended 121 added 20 equally spaced strikes on either side. The original strikes were preserved exactly, including their linear spacing in strike rather than log-moneyness.

This construction made two different comparisons possible. Original 81 versus Sparse 41 changed the count while retaining the endpoints. Central 41 versus Sparse 41 held the count at 41 while changing quote locations and span. At the larger noise limit, the mixture's mean full-domain density error was approximately 0.122286 for Central 41 and 0.062276 for Sparse 41. Their paired difference was 0.060010, with a Monte Carlo standard error of approximately 0.000637. Within this experiment, placing the same number of observations across a broader span made a material difference to recovery.

Every case was assessed on the same withheld price grid. Otherwise, a narrow-range fit could have appeared successful partly because its evaluation had also omitted the difficult wings. The clean-input Central 41 mixture control illustrated the issue: its price RMSE was approximately 0.118633 within the supplied quote span but 7.798099 over the common validation grid. This discrepancy did not require random errors. The lesson was to make the information comparison and evaluation domain explicit before interpreting changes in accuracy.

### 7.2 Extending the grid exposed a positivity problem in the noise model

The wider grid included very small call values. A uniform additive perturbation with an unchanged absolute limit could therefore produce negative synthetic prices. A diagnostic using the saved draws confirmed that the uncapped alternative at the 0.5-point limit would give 1,353 negative single-lognormal prices among 48,400 master-grid observations. This was a limitation of that proposed noise construction, not evidence that the clean option formula or density had become invalid.

The response was a declared bounded-noise model, Equation (26). Its half-width was the smaller of the selected amplitude and half the clean price. The perturbation remained symmetric and conditionally zero-mean, and generated prices stayed positive without being clipped afterwards. The bound was tested by independently integrating the error's mean and variance. Common random draws were preserved across patterns; deleting a strike did not generate a new perturbation for the retained observations.

The trade-off is substantive. The bound uses clean prices known only because this is a synthetic experiment, and it reduces errors on small option values. It is therefore an assumption to disclose, not a general solution for real quote quality. The updated notebook identifies this change from Milestone 6 and retains the effective error scales in the exports. The lesson was to keep data-generation assumptions visible when adapting a benchmark to a more demanding domain.

### 7.3 More repetitions required clearer uncertainty language

The study used 50 repetitions for each noisy scenario, with eight additional clean-input controls. This introduced three quantities that could easily have been confused: mean error against the known answer, the SD of individual fitted results and the Monte Carlo standard error of a reported average. As Morris, White and Crowther distinguish in their simulation-study paper, simulation uncertainty must be reported separately from estimator performance [16, §5.2]. Equations (27)–(29) formalised those summaries, including the finite-sample variance factor and the use of within-repetition differences for paired comparisons.

The mixture results showed why that distinction matters. At the 0.5-point limit, Central 41 had a smaller signed mean upper-tail error than Original 81, approximately 0.1061 versus 0.3108 percentage points. However, its across-repetition SD was larger, approximately 0.3794 versus 0.2333 percentage points. Its paired change in mean absolute tail error was approximately +0.0475 percentage points, with MCSE 0.0425. A lower signed bias alone therefore did not establish generally better tail recovery, and that last difference was imprecisely estimated relative to its size.

Extended 121 reduced the mixture's mean absolute upper-tail error relative to Original 81 by approximately 0.2632 percentage points, with paired MCSE 0.0338. Its mean fitted probability was approximately 1.1450%, compared with the known 1.1885%. This was an improvement under the declared design, with a remaining downward mean error. Because the extended pattern also added observations, the result was not presented as the isolated effect of range. The clean extended mixture control retained an error of approximately −0.0503 percentage points, showing that random quote perturbations were not necessary for the residual discrepancy.

The lesson was to display known values, individual repetitions, mean errors and uncertainty in the mean together. The clean-input result is also distinct from noise-averaged bias: constraints make the estimator nonlinear in its inputs, so its value at exact prices need not equal its average over perturbed prices.

### 7.4 Scaling up required reproducible inputs and complete case accounting

The complete batch contained 808 joint fits and 6,464 marginal densities. Each attempt was recorded with its inputs, solver history, probability weights and numerical checks. Separate random-generator child streams made the inputs independent of process scheduling. A code-and-protocol fingerprint and content checksum allowed completed cases to be reused while rejecting mismatched results. Aggregate performance required every planned repetition in the corresponding group; a failed case would remain visible rather than disappear from a success-only average.

All 808 planned fits passed the unchanged acceptance checks; no new solver failure was observed in this batch. At most seven calendar-refinement rounds were needed, and the worst final gap was approximately $-9.995\times10^{-10}$, within the $10^{-8}$ tolerance. Small signed mass residuals were retained, including a minimum raw mass of approximately $-1.33\times10^{-12}$ across solver rounds. No clipping or renormalisation was used. Successful computation did not remove the approximation errors, idealised noise assumptions or unresolved empirical-data requirements.

### 7.5 Numerical success did not guarantee a complete figure export

A final artifact check found that the standalone Figure 19 PNG was empty, although its embedded notebook copy remained intact. The numerical results were unaffected. The immediate cause of the empty file was not established, so it was not attributed to the estimator or claimed as a diagnosed plotting-library fault.

The intact notebook attachment provided a recoverable copy. The export routine was also changed to write a temporary image, verify that it could be read completely, and only then replace the destination file. SVG exports received an XML-structure check. Regenerating the figures and checking the exported PNGs against the notebook attachments completed the verification. The lesson was to test the delivered artifacts as well as the numerical results: a successful computation and a visible notebook image do not, by themselves, establish that the separate publication file is usable.

## 8. Notebook 8: selecting smoothing and questioning the supplied forward

### 8.1 A successful selection calculation exposed a mismatch between targets

As Cawley and Talbot explain in their model-selection study, assessment must include the selection procedure itself [17, §5.1]. The project therefore selected a coefficient independently in each noise realisation and reserved the known density and separate clean prices for evaluation. The candidate grid, fold rule and comparison measures were fixed before the full batch; the existing baseline was retained as a paired control.

The single-lognormal experiments selected the baseline coefficient in every repetition. The Original 81 mixture experiments instead selected the smallest candidate, $10^{-7}$, in all 20 repetitions. That change improved the noisy interior validation criterion, but mean clean-price RMSE over the declared common grid increased from approximately 0.8887 to 1.0928 index points. Mean full-density error also increased from 0.0574 to 0.0704, with a paired increase of approximately 0.01298 and MCSE 0.00073. The computation had completed correctly; the disappointing recovery comparison was a substantive finding.

A post-hoc diagnostic divided the unchanged clean test grid into its 640 prices inside the original quote range and its 320 outside it. The selected fit reduced mean inside-range RMSE from approximately 0.1571 to 0.1235 points, while outside-range RMSE rose from 1.5224 to 1.8847 points. The paired outside-range increase was approximately 0.3623 points, with MCSE 0.0653. This located the price deterioration in extrapolation. The diagnostic was explicitly labelled as added after observing the result; it neither changed the candidate grid nor selected a different fit.

Extended 121 provided a complementary result. The mixture again selected $10^{-7}$ in all 20 repetitions, reducing mean clean-price RMSE from approximately 0.1587 to 0.1174 points. Nevertheless, mean full-density error increased from 0.0524 to 0.0630. Here the test prices were inside the observed range, so the discrepancy could not be described solely as a consequence of extrapolating the price test grid. Price fit and density recovery remained distinct objectives even with the wider quote coverage.

The lesson was to match the interpretation to the quantity the rule actually targets. Interior-price selection does not directly observe the wings or the density itself. The small price-validation score could not justify describing the entire density as improved. The mean absolute one-year upper-tail error under Original 81 did decrease, by approximately 0.0692 percentage points with MCSE 0.0316, while full-density error increased. Reporting both prevents a favourable single risk metric from replacing the broader recovery assessment. Selecting a boundary candidate also establishes only a preference within three tested values, not an optimum beyond the grid.

### 8.2 Uneven validation folds required explicit observation weighting

Retaining both endpoints in every training fold preserved the quote span, but it left 79 or 119 interior quotes to distribute among three folds. The fold sizes were therefore unequal: 27/26/26 and 40/40/39. An unweighted mean of three fold-level errors would give an observation in a smaller fold more influence than one in a larger fold.

Equation (30) pools squared residuals and divides by the actual total observation count. An independent hand calculation checked this weighting, and a controlled fitter verified that each held-out quote was absent from its training inputs and was scored against its noisy observed value. This was a design risk addressed before reporting, rather than a claim that an incorrect averaging rule had first been used in the main study. The 20 quote realisations, rather than the three dependent folds, supplied the repeated units for the Monte Carlo summaries.

### 8.3 A forward stress could silently change the tail event

The earlier risk routine expressed thresholds relative to the forward stored with each fit. Reusing that interpretation during a forward stress would have compared different physical events: changing the forward would also move the level called “1.8F”. Likewise, using the perturbed forward to normalise the known benchmark would compare densities in inconsistent coordinates.

The evaluation therefore retained each stressed fit's physical bin edges and probabilities and used a separate, fixed reference forward when interpreting the distribution. Equation (32) states the change of variables. Independent integration checked the density error in physical coordinates, while a uniform-distribution example checked exact tail probabilities, mean, variance and unchanged option prices. The first toy fixture used one bin, but the existing pricing interface requires at least two. Splitting the same uniform distribution across two equal bins made the fixture valid and retained its exact analytical answers; no estimator code was altered to accommodate the test.

This distinction separated a changed fitted distribution from a changed question. The events $S_T<0.8F_{\mathrm{true}}$, $S_T>1.2F_{\mathrm{true}}$ and $S_T>1.8F_{\mathrm{true}}$ remained fixed in every stress. The support still moved in physical units because the fitter defines it in supplied-forward coordinates. That is an explicit consequence of the tested estimator, not an effect removed by the evaluation conversion.

### 8.4 Enforcing a supplied mean could expose incompatible price inputs

As Bahra's valuation framework makes clear, the forward is linked to the terminal mean [1]. Enforcing that identity with a supplied number does not independently confirm the number's accuracy. The deliberate forward stresses therefore kept the observed prices, strikes and discounts unchanged while perturbing the mean input. They also retained the smoothing coefficient chosen at the correct forward, making the scope of this sensitivity experiment explicit.

The positive-forward pilot generated much larger clean-price errors than the negative stress of the same size, despite passing the same numerical checks. The complete mixture experiment had mean clean-price RMSE of approximately 0.1174 points at the correct forward, 1.7653 at −0.5% and 10.2713 at +0.5%. Equation (33) provided an independently checkable diagnostic: raising the supplied forward raises the discounted intrinsic-value lower bound, and observed calls below that bound cannot be matched exactly by a valid distribution with that mean. At +0.5%, an average of 227.3 of the 968 mixture quotes lay below that lower bound. The full study retains the count, maximum and RMS of those shortfalls. No price was clipped to conceal the conflict. The diagnostic helps explain the asymmetry but does not isolate every simultaneous change in support and normalisation.

The practical lesson is that a mathematically consistent surface remains conditional on the supplied market inputs. The observed forward sensitivity strengthens the need to verify parity inputs and observation timing before empirical fitting. The imposed forward mean and calendar ordering are distinct from recovery accuracy. The chosen shock sizes are deliberate stresses, not measured probabilities of market-data error.

### 8.5 Repeated selection expanded the reproducibility requirements

Every selection experiment required nine training-only joint fits before full-data refitting and evaluation. Saving only the final density would have omitted the evidence for how its coefficient was chosen. The case records therefore retain fold membership, held-out residuals, every candidate's score, probability weights and solver histories. Forward cases identify the exact parent selection result; a changed parent checksum invalidates reuse. Identical baseline/selected fits are reused and counted once, and zero-shift stresses reuse the selected fits.

The full batch completed 1,000 unique joint fits and 8,000 marginals, with no failed or blocked experiment. These include 720 training-only CV fits, 80 selected full-data fits, 40 additional fixed-control fits and 160 nonzero forward stresses. The worst final calendar gap was approximately $-9.996\times10^{-10}$, within the unchanged $10^{-8}$ tolerance. At most seven refinement rounds and 40 solver iterations per round were required. All 55 project tests passed, including reconstruction of fold scores and fixed physical risk events from the saved probabilities.

The verified temporary-file figure export introduced after Notebook 7 was retained. Earlier notebooks and both interactive companions remain unchanged. The general lesson is to preserve enough intermediate evidence to reconstruct a methodological decision, while distinguishing a deliberate stress outcome from a failed solve or an invalid test fixture.

## 9. Notebook 9 preparation: a valid workflow did not supply the missing data

### 9.1 Source access and source suitability were separate setbacks

The empirical stage began with a renewed source assessment. Cboe's documented EOD product offered a possible snapshot source, but its advertised sample request again returned HTTP 403 [18]. The request record was saved; the sample contents were not inspected. A second provider advertised a public 2022 sample, but its own documentation described unsynchronised last-standing quotes and quote timestamps available only from August 2026 [19]. Downloading that sample would not have resolved this study's observation-time requirement. An institutional route was also identified through WRDS [20], but no authorised export or account access had been supplied.

The useful response was to separate the obtainable implementation work from the still-missing empirical evidence. The new bundle format records explicit observation and settlement datetimes, source evidence, discount observation and availability, and input checksums. A standalone runner can assess that bundle when it is available. Meanwhile, the entire route was exercised on a deliberately labelled synthetic fixture. None of these steps changes the original candidate's status or establishes a market result. The lesson was that documenting the remaining dependency can be a substantive result: replacing absent metadata with plausible assumptions would obscure the empirical limitation.

### 9.2 Inferring the forward introduced another route for held-out information

Earlier synthetic experiments supplied the forward as a known model input. For the empirical workflow it is inferred from call–put pairs using the parity relationship discussed by Aït-Sahalia and Lo [10]. That changes the information available to the estimator. Computing a forward from all quotes before holding some prices out would allow those held-out pairs to influence the training mean, support and scaling.

The correction was incorporated before reporting the fixture results: call and put at a strike are held out together, and each fold estimates its forward only from the remaining pairs. Equation (36) makes that dependency explicit. As Cawley and Talbot's discussion of model selection suggests [17], the procedure being assessed includes the preparatory choices estimated from the observations. An independent test changed a held-out call enough to change the full-data forward, while confirming that the corresponding fold's training forward and predictions stayed unchanged. This is a checked design precaution, not a claim that an earlier empirical result was contaminated; no earlier empirical fit exists.

### 9.3 Row validity, forward compatibility and spread reproduction differed

The matched-pair intersection in Equation (34) provides a conditional forward range. In a deliberate failure exercise, adding five points to the first call's bid and ask retained valid individual quote fields but made the intervals mutually inconsistent. The required uniform widening from Equation (35) was approximately 1.7234 forward points. The implementation reported that diagnostic and blocked calibration; it neither widened the quotes nor deleted the binding pair. The exercise demonstrates a conflict that a simple positive-bid/ask screen cannot detect.

The unmodified fixture completed ten joint fits over three horizons. It passed the mass, mean and continuous calendar checks at their stated tolerances. Yet one fitted put lay approximately 0.01993 index points outside its quoted interval, despite all input pairs admitting a common forward. This was retained as a separate outcome. The estimator minimises regularised call-midpoint residuals and does not impose bid/ask inequalities. Pairwise parity compatibility therefore does not guarantee that its chosen smooth density lies within every spread, and the observed residual does not prove that an alternative all-spread-feasible density is impossible.

Separate clean-price RMSEs were approximately 0.05257, 0.05195 and 0.03808 points for the nominal 60-, 120- and 240-day fixtures. Full-domain density errors were approximately 0.04960, 0.04411 and 0.06150. These are one example's synthetic recovery measures, not market accuracy estimates or a repeated comparison against the earlier studies. Known prices and densities entered evaluation only after selection and refitting. The general lesson is to report input consistency, solver acceptance, structural constraints, spread reproduction and density recovery as distinct questions.

### 9.4 Visual inspection caught a hidden residual

The first spread-residual plot used a fixed vertical range of ±1.5 half-spreads. Inspection against the numerical extrema showed that the failing put was approximately −2.05 half-spreads from its midpoint, so its marker lay outside that plotting window. The chart was regenerated using a common range covering every residual, with the outside-spread observation labelled explicitly. The prices, fitted weights and statistics were unchanged. This was a presentation correction with a methodological lesson: a chart can understate a documented failure even when the underlying calculations are correct.

The final fixture records preserve every fold's pair membership, inferred forward interval, probability weights, residuals and solver diagnostics. The original eight notebooks and both offline interactive companions are checked against the preceding cumulative release. The Figure 4 explanation remains beneath its original figure and caption. At the preparation handoff, the next dependency was still a verified market export and its documentation. The subsequently supplied marking-price files enabled the separate assessment described below; fixture success alone did not resolve that dependency.

### 9.5 The supplied files contained two price families and only two dates

The uploaded filenames looked like three candidate datasets, but the standard and late EOD files represented two times on 18 September, while the EOM file represented 31 August. Combining them would have confused time variation with additional contracts. The files also placed actual market and final indicative prices beside one another. Cboe's own documentation [21] resolved that field distinction. Independent downloads matched the uploaded hashes, and OSI contract fields and message dates were checked before fitting.

The adapter uses the actual bid/ask columns and preserves the indicative fields only for audit. All indicated sizes were one, without evidence that they represented actual depth. A new source-specific profile records market size as unavailable instead of manufacturing canonical NBBO/size observations. The earlier adapter and original candidate remain unchanged. The lesson is that a usable source can require a different, explicitly justified profile; it does not justify retrospectively changing the meaning of an earlier rejected field. The 15:15 snapshot and August date are reserved for subsequent comparisons.

### 9.6 Missing discount inputs were handled through an explicit estimated proxy

The new files did not contain the external curve required by the original workflow. As Aït-Sahalia and Lo's parity relationship shows [10], the call-minus-put price is linear in strike with slope equal to minus the discount. Equation (39) therefore estimates the discount and discounted forward together, while requiring the fitted parity line to remain inside every retained pair's bid–ask bounds. This extends the empirical model and its assumptions; it does not recover an independently observed risk-free curve.

The parameters were centred and scaled to avoid operating directly on a large intercept and strike values. Linear programmes measured the discount-feasibility range, and a separate pair-interval check verified the chosen forward. The primary sample's least-squares optima were already feasible; a deliberately constructed midpoint outlier separately exercised the constrained solver. A nonlinear incompatible three-pair fixture correctly stopped before calibration. Negative-rate-compatible discounts above one remained allowed. These checks distinguished an actual viable workaround from simply inserting a plausible interest rate.

Estimating another input also expanded the validation dependency. Both discount and forward were recomputed inside every training fold; changing a held-out pair left that fold's inputs and predictions unchanged. The resulting estimates remain conditional on the retained contracts, spreads and parity model. Their feasible ranges are not confidence intervals, and their uncertainty has not been jointly propagated into the density. For the November and December horizons, named-timezone conversion also retained the additional hour across the daylight-saving transition.

### 9.7 Synthetic tuning choices did not automatically suit the market sample

The first market run retained the three smoothing coefficients previously used in the synthetic procedure. Its nine training fits selected the weakest value, 1e−7. Although all structural and solver checks passed, 650 of 1,938 fitted option prices lay outside their quoted spreads, with a maximum miss of about 1.8661 points. The overlapping call-price curves could have concealed that discrepancy; the full half-spread residual plot made it visible.

This result motivated a separate lower-grid diagnostic at 1e−10, 1e−9 and 1e−8, saved with the primary result's checksum. It retained the original folds, support, input estimates and solver requirements. The diagnostic reduced the selected noisy held-out call-price score by about 60%, while still selecting the lowest tested value. It did not establish an interior optimum or independently validated improvement. The primary result remains in the notebook, and the diagnostic is explicitly labelled post-hoc throughout the figures, table and interactive explorer. As Cawley and Talbot explain [17], searching after seeing evaluation results changes what the same data can establish.

### 9.8 Improved call prediction still left put and tail limitations

The weaker diagnostic fit missed 501 spreads: only three calls, but 498 puts. A nearby coefficient missed 496 in total despite a worse call-price selection score. This is an observed mismatch between the tuning target and an additional evaluation target, rather than a failed convergence check. The loss function fits call midpoints; puts follow the same density and inferred parity line. Pairwise feasibility of that line does not imply that this selected call curve falls in the intersection of each call interval and its parity-transformed put interval. A joint call/put or all-spread feasibility formulation remains a concrete follow-up; no infeasibility of such an alternative has been established.

Extrapolated probabilities exposed a second limitation. The first two expiries lacked strikes reaching 1.2 times their inferred forwards, and none reached 1.8 times. The primary first-horizon probability above 1.8F was about 0.009674%, compared with about 0.000001186% under the weaker diagnostic. No known market distribution exists here against which to declare either answer correct. As Malz's discussion of extrapolation suggests [14], coverage and threshold placement must accompany any tail estimate. The useful lesson is to preserve the observed disagreement, rather than turn a numerically accepted surface into a precise tail forecast.

### 9.9 Failure reporting and presentation needed their own checks

A deliberate single-row OSI failure exposed a secondary exception: the audit attempted to summarise message ages when no valid timestamp had been recorded. The correction returns unavailable extrema while preserving the metadata rejection, allowing the runner to report the actual input problem. Valid-input outputs were unchanged. This checked failure path matters because a diagnostic should explain an invalid file, not itself fail before retaining the reason.

The numerical evidence now preserves 24 empirical joint fits and 72 marginals across the primary and diagnostic studies, separately from the preparation fixture. Every fit passes the stated continuous-calendar tolerance and strict solver status; these facts do not remove the price and tail limitations. Figures include full-support and enlarged density views, all spread residuals, captions below the images and explicit post-hoc labels. The third offline explorer links coefficient choices, three maturity curves, exact histogram slices and conditional risk summaries. Surface interpolation is labelled as a visual guide rather than calibrated intermediate maturities. The earlier eight notebooks, two explorers and original Figure 4 placement remain preserved.

## 10. Notebook 10: price compatibility, numerical limits and dated comparisons

### 10.1 Improving the call objective had not answered the put-spread problem

Notebook 9's weaker-smoothing diagnostic reduced call-price error but left 498 fitted puts outside their original spreads. This made another smoothing search an incomplete response to the observed problem. The new calculation instead translates each put interval through the inferred parity line and intersects it with its matched call interval, as stated in Equation (41). Cohen, Reisinger and Wang's bid–ask-aware arbitrage-repair discussion [23] informed the question about interval compatibility; their repair procedure was not substituted for our estimator.

The response was to separate two tasks: first establish whether the finite density family can satisfy all original call and put ranges, then choose a minimum-curvature member if it can. This gives an LP compatibility diagnostic followed by the hard-bound QP in Equation (43). No price was widened, deleted as a fitting outlier, or replaced by a theoretical value. The regularising criterion remains a modelling choice, but there is no fitted midpoint-error-versus-roughness coefficient in this formulation.

The three primary 240-bin fits reproduce all 5,518 retained option intervals at the stated counting tolerance: 1,680 August prices, 1,938 standard September prices and 1,900 late September prices. A 120-bin September control also reproduces all its spreads, showing that the changed criterion can resolve that sample's price-fit problem without doubling resolution. The lesson is specific: a loss function that rewards one kind of fit need not enforce every economically relevant check. The improvement is conditional in-sample compatibility, not proof of a correct density or better prediction.

### 10.2 A grid restriction could be mistaken for inconsistent market quotes

The late snapshot did not admit the same result on the original 120-bin representation. Its minimum uniform widening was approximately 0.026564 index points, even with the maturities fitted independently. Adding within-snapshot calendar restrictions did not create the incompatibility. On 240 bins with the same support, prices and carry, the LP returned zero widening and the strict hard-bound fit succeeded. The old support could therefore be retained while the density representation became more flexible.

The response was to adopt the 240-bin grid for all three primary snapshots and preserve the 120-bin controls, including the blocked one. The diagnostic widening was never applied to quotes. This was a decision after seeing all three snapshots in the pilot; the protocol says so and does not call any observation a blind time test. The lesson is that infeasibility belongs to the specified quotes, carry and mathematical representation together. It cannot, on its own, be presented as model-independent evidence of market arbitrage.

### 10.3 Equivalent mathematics behaved differently in finite precision

The first QP formulation expressed quote inequalities in index points. The standard September case stopped at `AlmostSolved` under the required $10^{-11}$ tolerances. Multiplying the objective by 0.1, 10 or 100 still failed that strict status. These unsuccessful trials remain in `comparison_qp_pilot.json` and `comparison_qp_scaling_pilot.json`; they were not retrospectively counted as successful fits.

Dividing each quote row and its bounds by the corresponding inferred forward provided a useful reformulation. This leaves the mathematical inequality unchanged, while bringing its numerical scale closer to the probability restrictions. Together with explicit roughness residual variables, it allowed all three primary fits to reach the strict status. As Goulart and Chen explain in their Clarabel paper [15], numerical acceptance is tied to stated feasibility and optimality criteria; a different formulation must still be checked against the original problem.

That last step exposed another setback. The first full run's standard September 120-bin control reported `Solved`, yet an independent check found a $2.97\times10^{-7}$-point quote violation, above the declared $10^{-7}$ limit. The original run stopped and its log was retained. The runner was changed to save the failed candidate and try equivalent positive quote-row multipliers of 1, 10 and 100, without relaxing the tolerance. Multiplier 10 resolved this case. The largest accepted training-spread residual across all accepted cases is about $8.18\times10^{-8}$ points. The lesson is to check results in the units of the scientific question as well as trusting the solver's internal status.

### 10.4 One validation fold remains a numerical limitation

The same response did not resolve every case. The first late-snapshot holdout fold was feasible in the LP, but its first QP attempt had a $1.29\times10^{-7}$-point physical-price miss despite `Solved` status. The two larger row multipliers returned `AlmostSolved`. No candidate met every declared requirement, so this fold remains unaccepted. The numerical limit is explicit; it is not labelled as quote infeasibility and no failed score is substituted with a successful full-sample score.

All calls and puts at a held strike were excluded together, and discount and forward were re-estimated on training pairs only. The accepted August folds missed 40 of 1,668 held-out price intervals; the standard September folds missed 46 of 1,926. The two accepted late-snapshot folds missed 24 of 1,256. Its incomplete two-of-three coverage is shown next to its score rather than hidden in an average. As Cawley and Talbot's evaluation discussion [17] indicates, reuse of inspected evidence limits performance claims; these folds are descriptive strike-interpolation checks, not temporal prediction tests.

There were 18 planned fit cases: 16 accepted QPs, one grid-incompatible control and one numerically unresolved fold. That accounting accompanies the 48 accepted marginal fits. The lesson is that an honest empirical analysis can be complete while still containing a documented failed case. Completion requires stating what was attempted and what its evidence can support, rather than changing a threshold until every row is successful.

### 10.5 Zero in-sample spread misses did not identify the tails

The chosen smooth densities sometimes assigned nearly zero mass to remote upper-tail events. Treating these small numbers as well determined would have repeated an earlier mistake: confusing a selected curve with information uniquely supplied by the quotes. The response was to drop the roughness preference and solve minimum and maximum probability LPs over all densities satisfying the same quote, probability and calendar restrictions. Seventy-two extrema cover three events at each actual maturity and at the assumed common horizon, across the three snapshots.

For the standard September 60-day mixture, the chosen probability above 1.8 in normalised coordinates is about 0.0002990%, while compatible values extend from 0 to approximately 0.0157987%. For August the chosen value rounds almost to zero, but its feasible upper bound is about 0.0185933%. As Malz's extrapolation discussion [14] suggests, an apparently precise tail output can depend heavily on the chosen continuation beyond observed strikes. Figure 32 makes the distinction visible with a point estimate inside a conditional feasible range.

These are not statistical confidence intervals. The LPs keep the full-sample carry, finite support, grid and interpolation rule fixed, so they do not measure every source of uncertainty or establish sharp bounds over all distributions. The independent LP price-residual allowance is $2\times10^{-6}$ points, separately recorded from the stricter QP counting tolerance. The lesson is to state both the family over which uncertainty is explored and what remains outside it.

### 10.6 Comparing dates introduced a horizon problem

The files contain two observation dates and a second September snapshot, rather than three dates. The same October, November and December expiries shorten by 18 days between August and standard September, and by another 15 minutes at the later observation. Plotting the same expiry across dates therefore mixes a change in observations with the passage of time. The changing inferred forwards also mean that a fixed normalised threshold need not be the same index-level event.

The response was to keep the fixed-expiry comparison, with its elapsed-day labels, and add an explicitly assumed 60-day normalised mixture between adjacent maturity distributions. Equation (45) preserves mass, mean and linear option valuation in those coordinates. It does not extrapolate, invent a 60-day forward, or turn an unquoted horizon into a direct observation. A separate common-contract control intersects strikes across all three snapshots and re-estimates carry on that subset, exposing sensitivity to quote composition and carry together.

The August-to-standard-September density distance is about 0.048225 at the common horizon and 0.051226 in the common-contract control. Neither is an estimation error or a measure of statistical significance. The conditional lower-tail ranges overlap. The lesson is to choose comparison coordinates and horizons explicitly before interpreting changes; no particular news event or change in physical expectations is inferred from this small sample.

### 10.7 Dynamic presentation needed to preserve these distinctions

The fourth offline explorer adds observed-snapshot playback, shared plot scales, date-specific 3D surfaces and exact histogram slices. Its 60-day line is labelled as interpolated. When the common-contract control is selected, it suppresses the all-pair feasible ranges because those bounds use different carry and quote restrictions. Playback advances through only the three available observations, without manufacturing a smooth history between dates.

The numerical and presentation checks also need the complete batch. A verification run started while results were still being written initially saw only part of the snapshot set and no final code-hash record. Waiting for the calculation to finish resolved those incomplete-record checks; the numerical routines were not changed to satisfy them. The complete suite now passes 84 tests, including independent uniform-density and deliberately nonconvex-price examples, saved extrema, holdout membership and training-only carry reconstruction. The nine earlier notebooks and three earlier explorers remain byte-for-byte preserved, including the Figure 4 explanation below its plot and caption.

Offline Chromium checks verified all three snapshots against saved numerical values, the common-horizon line, common-contract bound suppression, keyboard expiry selection, observed-date playback, rotation, zoom and hover. The 390-pixel and 320-pixel layouts stayed within the page, with the wide risk table scrolling inside its panel. No JavaScript errors or external HTTP requests were recorded.

## 11. Implications for the final paper

Across the notebooks, different evidence was needed to establish whether a computation had converged, whether its output satisfied the imposed restrictions, whether it recovered a known distribution, how precisely its repeated performance had been measured, and whether the available data supported an empirical interpretation. Progress on one question did not automatically answer the others. Clean-data differentiation, constrained fitting, calendar refinement and the sensitivity studies each addressed a different part of that sequence.

The most useful responses exploited structure that could be checked: exact interval-payoff integration, analytical locations of within-bin minima, a matched independent control, paired noise across benchmarks and a smoothing coefficient adjusted for grid spacing. Known synthetic answers made errors measurable while the market-data questions remained unresolved. Preserving failed diagnostics and documenting unsuccessful solver adjustments also made the scope of the eventual result clearer.

Repeated quote errors, alternative strike coverage, smoothing selection and supplied-forward sensitivity have been tested under declared synthetic models. Notebook 9 adds the first observed SPXW calibration; Notebook 10 resolves the retained full-sample spread misses within its finer density family, compares the archived observations and measures conditional tail nonuniqueness. It also retains a numerically unresolved validation fold and nonzero held-out spread errors. Two dates enable a small comparison but do not provide a long-run historical validation sample. All ten planned analytical stages are now documented. The final paper preserves these distinctions and limitations while combining the research question, evidence, numbered results, scholarly references and requested front matter into a coherent argument.
